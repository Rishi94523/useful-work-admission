1. Exact verification over a finite field on quantized models — my top pick. The current scheme's weakest point is the float tolerance: honest float32 drift forces rtol=1e-3, which opens a "subtle drift" cheating band that only spot audits cover. If the model is quantized to int8/int16 (industry-standard anyway), the entire computation becomes integer-exact, and the projection check becomes Freivalds over ℤ_p — zero tolerance, soundness exactly bounded at ~1/p per projection, no audits needed at all, and every cross-language float headache (your toFixed(4) canonicalization) disappears because browsers and the server agree bit-for-bit. "Tolerance-free verifiable quantized inference in untrusted browsers" is a clean, defensible contribution and would be the strongest claim in both the paper and a patent.

2. Batched Freivalds with cheater localization. Take B submissions of the same layer, draw random coefficients c_i, and check one combined projection r·(Σcᵢzᵢ) ≈ s·(Σcᵢxᵢ) + (Σcᵢ)(r·b). One O(in+out) check now covers the whole batch; if it fails, group-testing (binary search over subsets) localizes the cheater in O(log B) extra checks. Verification cost per submission becomes sublinear — that transforms your economics story at scale, and I haven't seen it applied to a PoUW stream.

3. Transformer support — and this is the real "industry models" unlock. Attention looks non-affine (QKᵀ is a product of two client-computed matrices), but it decomposes perfectly: Q, K, V are affine in the block input (verifiable as-is); then QKᵀ is a matrix product of two submitted-and-verified matrices, which is literally Freivalds' original problem — check S·r ≈ Q·(Kᵀ·r) in O(seq·d). Softmax and LayerNorm are cheap row-wise server-replayed post-ops, exactly like your relu/maxpool. PV is another matrix-product check. So a full transformer block verifies with the primitive you already have plus one new check type. That gets you to LLM-era labeling tasks (the kind ScaleAI actually sells now), and the cheap non-zk version of this is far less explored than zkML.

4. Leakage-budgeted projection rotation. A reviewer will ask: the accept/reject decision is an oracle about the secret r — an adaptive attacker submitting crafted z's learns a halfspace constraint per rejection. Quantify the leakage (bits per rejection), set a rotation budget (rotate r after N failures per layer), and you've turned a vulnerability question into an analysis section. Cheap to implement, makes the security story rigorous.

5. Peer attestation. Have the next solver in the pipeline recompute a small server-chosen secret slice (a few output coordinates) of the previous segment as part of their task. Server compares the two untrusted parties; disagreement triggers a real audit. Audits become nearly free for the server, and cheating then requires collusion between randomly-paired strangers. Nice systems contribution with a clean collusion-threshold analysis.
